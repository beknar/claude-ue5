implement these quests for the locations above:
Hunting and combat

Monster bounty: Track and kill a named beast that’s been attacking travelers, following carcasses, tracks and torn camps to its territory. Twist: it was driven out of its home by something worse.
Lair clearing: Wipe out a bandit camp, goblin warren or wyvern roost that’s choking a trade road. Twist: the “bandits” are displaced villagers.
Legendary hunt: A multi-stage hunt for an apex creature, using bait, traps and its weak points, with a trophy or crafting material as the reward. Twist: a rival hunting party is after it too.
Infestation: A spreading threat like giant spiders, rats or fungal crawlers that keeps coming back until the source (a nest, queen or blighted tree) is destroyed.
Hold the pass: Defend a bridge, ford or mountain chokepoint against waves of attackers until help arrives or the refugees are through.

Exploration and discovery

Survey and cartography: Map an uncharted region by climbing vantage points, naming landmarks and finding water sources, for a guild or crown.
Lost ruin: Find a site from fragmentary directions, an old legend or a half-burned map, then survive what’s inside.
Buried cache: Treasure maps, smugglers’ stashes or a dead adventurer’s hoard, with riddles keyed to landmarks.
Lost expedition: Follow an earlier party’s trail through abandoned camps and journal pages to learn what happened to them.
Trailblazing: Find a safe route through mountains, marsh or forest for a caravan or army, then guide them through it.

Gathering and taming

Rare ingredient: Collect a flower that blooms only at midnight, during a storm or in one season, often in a dangerous spot.
Live capture: Bring a creature back alive for a menagerie, a scholar or a breeding program, which means nonlethal tactics, cages and keeping it fed.
Tame a mount: Earn the trust of a wild griffon, horse or great elk through a series of trials, feedings and chases.

Rescue and escort

Search and rescue: Find a missing child, hunter or lost patrol before nightfall or before the weather turns.
Wilderness escort: Protect pilgrims, a caravan or someone who can’t fight on a dangerous crossing.
Manhunt: Track an escaped prisoner, deserter or thief. Twist: they’re innocent, or running from something.
Urgent delivery: Carry medicine, a message or a relic across hostile land before a deadline.

Survival

Stranded: After a shipwreck, an ambush or a fall, the party has lost its gear and has to find water, shelter and food and make its way back.
Weather the disaster: A blizzard, wildfire or flash flood hits, and the party has to get people to safety or simply survive it.

Mystery and supernatural

Investigation: Livestock mutilations, vanishing travelers or strange lights in the woods, with clues spread across the area that point to the real cause.
Restless spirit: Lay a haunting to rest at a lonely grave, battlefield or drowned village by settling its unfinished business.
Blight cleansing: Rot, corruption or a poisoned spring is spreading. Find the source and purify or destroy it.
Sacred site: Restore a shrine, relight a beacon or perform a rite at standing stones at the solstice, with guardians or rivals in the way.

Factions and frontier

Territory dispute: Druids against loggers, two clans fighting over a valley, or settlers against fey. The party can mediate, pick a side or play them against each other.
Frontier outpost: Found, supply and defend a new settlement or watchtower, which unfolds into a chain of smaller quests.





add these ruin types in the wilderness:
Temple ruins: Religious complexes, often the best-surviving buildings of a civilization because they were built in stone to last. Think toppled columns, empty altars and processional avenues, as at Karnak or the Parthenon.
Castle and fortress ruins: Broken curtain walls, hollow keeps and filled-in moats. Many were “slighted,” meaning deliberately wrecked by the winning side so they couldn’t be used again.
Abbey and monastery ruins: Roofless Gothic naves with empty window tracery. In England, many date from Henry VIII’s Dissolution of the Monasteries, when the lead roofs were stripped and sold.
Jungle-swallowed cities: Settlements taken back by forest, with tree roots prying apart walls and vines covering the façades. Ta Prohm at Angkor and Tikal are classic examples.
Tells: Artificial hills built up over thousands of years as each generation built on the rubble of the last. Digging into one is like reading a timeline backward, as at Troy or Megiddo.
Barrows and burial mounds: Earthen or stone mounds raised over burial chambers. They range from Neolithic long barrows to the kurgans of the Eurasian steppe.
Megalithic monuments: Standing stones, stone circles, dolmens and long stone rows put up by prehistoric peoples. Their purpose is often debated, as at Stonehenge or Carnac.
Rock-cut architecture: Tombs, temples and façades carved straight into living rock rather than built. Petra and the Ellora caves are the standout examples.
Cliff dwellings: Multi-room settlements built into overhangs and alcoves in canyon walls, hard to reach and easy to defend. Mesa Verde is the best known.
Underground cities and catacombs: Tunnels, chambers and burial galleries dug below ground for shelter, defense or burial. Examples include Derinkuyu in Cappadocia and the catacombs of Rome and Paris.
Submerged ruins: Towns sunk by ground subsidence, rising seas or earthquakes, like Baiae and Pavlopetri. Villages drowned by modern reservoirs belong here too, and some resurface during droughts.
Ash-buried cities: Places sealed almost instantly by volcanic ash or mud, which preserved a snapshot of daily life down to graffiti and bread loaves. Pompeii and Herculaneum are the famous ones.
Sand-buried cities: Desert settlements slowly covered by drifting dunes after their water or trade routes failed. Many Silk Road oasis towns ended this way.
Amphitheaters and theaters: Huge public entertainment structures. Their size made them useful as quarries, fortresses or even housing later on: medieval Arles had a whole neighborhood inside its amphitheater.
Infrastructure ruins: Aqueducts, bridges, roads, harbors and frontier walls such as Pont du Gard and Hadrian’s Wall. They often outlast the cities they served.
Ghost towns: Fairly recent settlements abandoned when the mine dried up, the railroad moved or the economy collapsed. They tend to be eerily intact, with furniture still inside, as at Bodie, California or Kolmanskop, Namibia.
Industrial ruins: Derelict mills, factories, smelters, mineheads and power stations. They’re a favorite of urban explorers and photographers.
War ruins and memorials: Structures destroyed in conflict and deliberately left that way as reminders. Examples include the Hiroshima Peace Memorial dome, the old Coventry Cathedral and the martyred village of Oradour-sur-Glane.
Exclusion-zone ruins: Places abandoned suddenly because of disaster or contamination, then left to decay with nature moving back in. Pripyat near Chernobyl is the archetype.
Follies (sham ruins): Fake ruins built on purpose, mostly in 18th-century landscape gardens, to look romantic and ancient. Some included crumbling towers and broken arches that were never whole.
